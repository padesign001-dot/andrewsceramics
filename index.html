The work

Somewhere between control and collapse

Forty years as a designer.
None of it prepares you for clay.

I spent most of my career on a grid — control, precision, repeatability. Porcelain doesn’t take instruction. It sags, it cracks, it moves after you’ve already decided it’s finished.

Nothing in this work is ever perfect. Getting comfortable with that took longer than it should have.

Each piece — bowl or lily — takes about 25 hours to coax into shape: forming, refining, and firing, at a community studio in Nashville.

How they're made

It's a slow process.

1
Wedging

Porcelain is wedged until all the bubbles are gone and the clay becomes elastic, responsive, and ready.

2
Forming

All the pieces are hand-formed, folded, and curved. The way the porcelain behaves is different every time.

3
Refining

Walls are smoothed and thinned, edges are softened. Some will break, some will be abandoned. The best ones make it into the kiln.

4
Drying

The slowest step. Drying happens over days, under plastic. Rush it and the walls crack before they ever reach the kiln.

5
Firing

Typically I'll just bisque fire the pieces. Unglazed porcelain has a softness that doesn't need any embellishments.
